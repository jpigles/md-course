Molecular dynamics, from Newton's law to free energies

A self-study course on how molecular dynamics simulations work: the equations of motion, the force fields, the statistical mechanics, and the mathematics behind each, with lectures to watch, texts to read, live demos and exercises.

What this course is

Molecular dynamics is easy to run and hard to understand. A tutorial will get you a trajectory in an afternoon. Knowing why the time step is 2 fs, what the thermostat does to your ensemble, why a cutoff on electrostatics is wrong, what a force field's parameters mean, and how many independent samples are in your trajectory takes longer, and it is what decides whether the result can be trusted.

This course goes through all of it in order, with the derivations written out. It assumes you can differentiate and integrate, have seen vectors and matrices, and can read Python. Everything else is built up from Newton's second law.

Each module has the same layout:

  1. Goals: what you will be able to do at the end.
  2. Watch first: lectures on YouTube for that topic, from full university courses down to short explainers.
  3. The lesson: the ideas and the mathematics, step by step, with worked numbers.
  4. Try it: in four modules, a simulation that runs in the page.
  5. Check yourself: short questions with answers hidden until you click.
  6. Exercises: things to derive, code and run.
  7. Read and Key papers: where to go deeper, with free sources marked.

Tick "I have finished this module" at the bottom of each page and the sidebar keeps track of your progress (stored in your browser only).

Syllabus

#ModuleWhat you will be able to doHours
Part I · Foundations
1What molecular dynamics is, and what it can tell youDescribe the MD algorithm in six lines, say which physics it keeps and which it throws away, and name the three kinds of error every simulation has.4
2Classical mechanics for simulatorsGo from Newton to Lagrange to Hamilton, and understand phase space, conservation laws, Liouville's theorem and time reversibility, the four properties a good integrator must respect.6
3Statistical mechanics, the link between a trajectory and a measurementDerive the Boltzmann distribution, connect partition functions to free energies, and understand why a time average over an MD trajectory equals a thermodynamic average.8
Part II · The engine
4Integrating the equations of motionDerive the Verlet family of integrators twice (from Taylor series and from the Liouville operator), and understand why a simple second-order method beats higher-order ones for molecular dynamics.6
5Force fields I, the energy function term by termWrite the standard biomolecular energy function, know what each term represents physically and what typical parameter values are, and derive the force from each term.6
6Force fields II, where the parameters come from and which to useUnderstand how charges, Lennard-Jones and torsion parameters are fitted, how the main protein force fields and water models differ, and what was changed to make them work for disordered proteins.6
7Periodic boundaries, cutoffs and Ewald summationSimulate a small box that behaves like bulk liquid, cut the short-range interactions correctly, and derive the Ewald sum that makes long-range electrostatics exact.7
8Thermostats and barostats, controlling temperature and pressureUnderstand how each thermostat and barostat modifies the equations of motion, which of them sample the correct ensemble, and which to use for equilibration and for production.7
9Constraints, freezing the fastest motionsDerive constrained dynamics with Lagrange multipliers, understand how SHAKE, RATTLE, SETTLE and LINCS solve it, and know what hydrogen mass repartitioning and virtual sites do to the time step.4
Part III · Practice
10Running a simulation from a PDB file to a production trajectoryCarry out the full workflow (prepare, solvate, neutralise, minimise, equilibrate, produce) in GROMACS and in OpenMM, knowing what every step and every setting is for.8
11Analysing trajectories and putting error bars on the resultsCompute the standard structural and dynamical observables, and above all estimate their statistical uncertainty from correlated data.8
Part IV · Free energy and sampling
12Free energy I, potentials of mean force, umbrella sampling, WHAM and MBARCompute a free-energy profile along a coordinate with umbrella sampling, derive the WHAM and MBAR equations that combine the windows, and turn a profile into a binding constant.9
13Free energy II, alchemical methods and non-equilibrium workDerive free-energy perturbation, thermodynamic integration and the Bennett acceptance ratio, set up thermodynamic cycles for relative and absolute binding free energies, and understand the Jarzynski and Crooks relations.8
14Enhanced sampling, getting over barriersUnderstand the main families of enhanced sampling (replica exchange, metadynamics, adaptive biasing, accelerated and steered MD, weighted ensemble), derive their key equations, and choose between them.8
Part V · Beyond all-atom
15Coarse-grained models, from Martini to Gō models and implicit solventUnderstand what a coarse-grained potential is in statistical-mechanical terms, how the main model families are built, and what each can and cannot predict.8
16Kinetics, from rate theory to Markov state modelsRelate free-energy barriers to rates with transition state and Kramers theory, understand the committor, and build and validate a Markov state model from many short trajectories.7
17Machine learning in molecular simulationUnderstand how neural-network potentials are built and trained, how machine learning is used for coarse-graining and for generating equilibrium ensembles directly, and how to judge these tools with the statistics from the rest of the course.7

A twelve-week plan

About eight to ten hours a week. Do the exercises: the derivations only stick when you have reproduced them.

Week Modules Milestone
1 1, 2 You have run and read a 35-line MD program and can write Hamilton's equations
2 3 You can derive the Boltzmann distribution and compute an average from a partition function
3 4 You have derived velocity Verlet twice and measured energy conservation against time step
4 5, 6 You can read a topology file and say where each parameter came from
5 7 You have derived the Ewald sum and know every non-bonded setting in an input file
6 8, 9 You have added a Langevin thermostat and SHAKE to your own code
7 10 You have taken a protein from a PDB file to a production run, three repeats
8 11 Your results have error bars from block averaging and from repeats
9 12 You have written WHAM and converted a free-energy profile to a dissociation constant
10 13, 14 You have computed a hydration free energy and run a replica-exchange or metadynamics simulation
11 15, 16 You have run a coarse-grained model and built a small Markov state model
12 17, projects You have completed one of the capstone projects

The backbone texts

Four books cover almost everything here at greater depth, and they complement each other: Frenkel and Smit, Understanding Molecular Simulation (algorithms and why they work); Tuckerman, Statistical Mechanics: Theory and Molecular Simulation (the rigorous derivations); Allen and Tildesley, Computer Simulation of Liquids (practical detail and code); and Leach, Molecular Modelling (force fields and applications). Free alternatives that go a long way: M. Scott Shell's lecture notes for his UCSB simulation course, David Tong's Cambridge notes on classical dynamics and statistical physics, the GROMACS reference manual, and the best-practice articles of the Living Journal of Computational Molecular Science. All are listed with links in the library.

About the sources

The video, book and paper lists were compiled in November 2026. Every YouTube link was checked to resolve and its title and channel recorded from YouTube itself; every paper's bibliographic details were taken from the Crossref record for its DOI. The one-line descriptions of videos were written from their titles and course pages, not from watching each one in full, so treat them as a guide. Books are linked to publisher or author pages. A resource marked free is free from its author, publisher or an open repository.

The lesson text is original and is meant to be read alongside those sources, not instead of them. If you find an error, the source files for every page are in the repository.
